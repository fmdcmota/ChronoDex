## Summary
On 18 April 1881 the British Museum (Natural History) opened free to all in South Kensington, in a Romanesque building designed by Alfred Waterhouse. The Times called it a 'true Temple of Nature,' though The Field thought it more suited to a suburban tea-garden. Richard Owen, the first superintendent, wanted a 'cathedral to nature' anyone could visit free of charge. The record for local dress, street lighting and traffic in 1881 is thin, so those details are not filled in here.

## Fun facts
- The museum opened on 18 April 1881 as the British Museum (Natural History); it was not formally renamed the Natural History Museum until 1992.
- Alfred Waterhouse designed the building in a Romanesque style with an iron frame and brick and terracotta walls; the terracotta came from Gibbs and Canning of Tamworth.
- The revised tender was £352,000, and the contractors failed in the summer of 1879 while the museum was nearly finished.
- The site was the former site of the 1862 International Exhibition, demolished after Parliament vetoed the purchase of the exhibition building in July 1863.
- Richard Owen, the first superintendent, wanted a 'cathedral to nature' that anyone could visit free of charge.
- The transfer of collections from Bloomsbury was not fully complete until 1883.

## What the camera sees
Facing north from Cromwell Road, about 45 metres south of the museum, the view is filled by the newly opened Waterhouse façade: a long, symmetrical terracotta front with a central gable and twin towers, set back behind a forecourt with iron railings and a gate lodge. The building is complete externally and open, but the gardens are still incomplete, with only the path layout achieved and limited planting. The central gable is surmounted by a single figure of Adam.

Persists: The Waterhouse front, front lodge, gates and railings still survive.
Altered: The setting is now a busy arterial road and the museum is surrounded by much later buildings.
Not yet built: The Whale Hall of 1929–31.; The former Geology Museum/Earth Galleries of 1929–33.; The Darwin Centre of 1999–2008.; The pedestrian subway under Exhibition Road, largely completed in 1885.
Gone: The 1862 International Exhibition building, already demolished after the 1863 veto.

Surroundings: North: the museum itself fills the immediate view; beyond it, the central axis of the building is aligned with the Royal Albert Hall and the Albert Memorial further north. East: the South Kensington Museum — now the Victoria and Albert Museum — lies east along Cromwell Road/Exhibition Road. South: Cromwell Road and the residential terraces and stucco houses of the newly developed South Kensington estate. West: Gloucester Road, its station and the surrounding residential streets and mews. Immediate foreground: the museum forecourt, railings and gate lodge.
